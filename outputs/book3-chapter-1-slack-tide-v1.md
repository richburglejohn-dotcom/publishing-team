# Chapter 1: Slack Tide

At eleven-forty on a Tuesday night, the last ticket had been spiked, the lot off Magazine Street was empty, and the convection ovens had gone back to doing what they did best, which was hold a number and refuse to discuss it.

Baron Vieux had been back in New Orleans for three months and still hadn't decided whose kitchen he was standing in.

It was his. The idea was his, the steel was his, the name on the right side of the gray coat hanging in the back was his. But Theron ran it now, and not as a caretaker or a favor. He ran it the way a man runs a room he has earned. His own name was stitched on his chest in the same thread Baron's had once been. The line moved to his timing. The cooks said *Chef* to Theron without looking up, and said *Chef* to Baron looking straight at him, which was kinder and much worse.

Baron had come home to a restaurant that did not need him. It was exactly what he had built it to be. It turned out to be harder to stand inside than he'd imagined.

So he worked the late hours. He broke down stations nobody had asked him to break down. He re-labeled shelves that were correctly labeled. On Tuesdays, when the lot ran slow, he and Theron ate last, standing at the pass with whatever the day's final tray had given them, which was the oldest ritual the container held. It had started on a sidewalk on Decatur Street before either of them knew what it was. You talked about the shift while it was still warm.

Tonight the last tray was redfish.

Two fillets, skin-on, laid on a sheet pan and finished in the oven under a lacquer of Scarlet Elixir. The glaze was nearly black in the pan and red only where the light caught it, and it smelled of smoked guajillo and Peychaud's and a patience Baron had never explained to anyone. It had never been written down. It lived in his hands and in the particular way he stood at a stove when it was close.

Wedged behind the ticket rail since Friday was a cream-colored envelope from a law office on Poydras. Neither of them had mentioned it. Theron glanced at it twice an hour. Baron had stopped glancing on Saturday, which was not the same as no longer seeing it. He knew roughly what it said. He had decided, on a Tuesday, not to know exactly.

On the shelf above dry storage sat a scratched radio that Theron had carried in his first week as chef. Nobody had ever had the heart or the authority to remove it. It played the late jazz hours low enough to be furniture.

"You're hovering," Theron said, not turning from the board where he was wiping down a quarter-sheet pan.

"I'm checking the skin."

"You're hovering at it."

Baron folded a dry towel into thirds, opened the oven door, and the heat came out to meet him like a hand on the chest. He slid the pan toward himself and tipped it a few degrees into the light to listen with his eyes. The skin had set the way it should. Cracked, glassy, ready to give that sound.

He tapped it with a fingernail.

*There.*

The radio announcer's voice came in from the shelf, warm, tired, and local, riding out the end of a brass-band number.

"—that was the Tremé Collective, and next, a new one from a woman who learned to hold a room in the Quarter's dive bars and has been holding bigger ones ever since. Olivia Marigny. This is 'Slack Tide.'"

Theron's hand went to the dial. It was a slow movement, and an apologetic one.

"Leave it," Baron said.

He meant it. He had decided, around the second week home, that he would not become a man who flinched at a radio. He would hear her the way you hear weather.

The horn line came first. Two trumpets and a low reed, a little behind the beat, with a Caribbean lilt under it that the Quarter had never taught anybody. Then the voice.

It was not a voice he had never heard. That was the trouble. It was the same voice that had once come drifting off a side street and made him write a note in ink in a notebook. It was the same voice that had hummed in his head through the rest of a long walk home. Now it came out of a forty-dollar radio over the bones of a restaurant he had built, a little richer than he remembered and a little further away.

*Slack tide, slack tide,*
*nobody pulling, nobody pushed—*

He had not heard this song.

He turned his head the width of a breath.

That was all it took. A decade of kitchens had taught him that a breath was the whole margin, and he had spent it. The pan, tilted for the light, lipped. A bright dark tongue of Elixir slid over the rim and down the back of his left hand and across the wrist, where the towel stopped.

For the first second it was only information. Heat arriving at the wrong address.

Then it was not information.

He jerked, which was the wrong thing, the thing he had trained forty line cooks never to do. The pan swung. His weight went with it. His left hand flew out for the steel of the counter to catch himself, and the counter was not where his hand landed. His own knife was, the long chef's knife he had left on the board an arm's length from the oven, with the edge turned out toward the room. He had written that rule himself. Blade to the wall, spine to the cook.

The cut was clean and almost courteous. It took the heel of his palm in a single quiet line.

The pan hit the floor and rang. Somewhere above the ringing, a woman went on singing about water.

"*Baron.*"

Theron was already there, his shoulder under Baron's, the dry towel already around the wrist and the cut and, God help them both, the burn. "Don't — no. Sit. Sit down. Let me see it."

"Water," Baron said. His voice sounded far off and oddly instructional. "Burn gets cool water. Twenty minutes. Cut gets pressure."

"I know. You made me take the class."

"Then you know you can't do both with one towel."

"I know that too." Theron walked him to the hand sink, put the back of the hand and the wrist under a cold running tap, and with his other palm pressed the folded towel hard against the heel of Baron's hand. He did it without being told, and he did it right. Baron looked down at the two of them, a grown man being managed under a faucet by his cousin, and thought clearly, almost happily, *I ran that training.*

Theron reached up with his elbow and killed the radio.

The kitchen went quiet in a way it never had. The hood fans spun down. The last bar of the song stayed in the room for a second, like the smell of something burnt.

"Truck," Theron said. "Now."

"The pan—"

"Is on the floor, Chef. It can stay there."

---

The emergency department was a long, low room that smelled of floor wax and electrical heat. Baron had driven past the hospital for twenty years and had never imagined its inside.

They waited forty minutes, with the hand wrapped in a clean towel and propped on Baron's chest like something he was carrying for someone else. Theron slumped in the next chair, still in his coat, the Elixir drying dark on his sleeve.

At ten past one, a door opened.

"Vieux?"

She said it correctly, the way the city said it, a little softer than the spelling. He noticed because nobody did.

She was young, thirty or a little more, in navy scrubs, with a stethoscope draped around her neck and her dark hair pulled back severe and neat. The badge clipped at her chest read **KAY DORGENOIS, RN**. She looked at the hand, then at the towel, then at his face. It took about two seconds. It was the look he knew from the inside, the one that sorts a room and finishes before the room knows it was sorted.

"Back here," she said. "Your friend can stay out here and keep the chairs warm."

"Cousin," Theron said, and sat.

She put him in a curtained bay and turned on a light. "Walk me through what happened."

"A pan. A sauce. A knife."

"That's the headline. Give me the story."

He told it plainly: the oven, the tilt, the glaze, the knife on the board. He left out the radio. She unwound the towel with her thumb and forefinger, very gently, and he watched her read the damage the way he read a sear.

"What was in the pan?"

"A reduction."

"Sugar in it?"

"Cane sugar. Some."

"That's why it's deeper than it looks." She turned the wrist toward the light. The skin from the back of his hand to the edge of the wrist had gone shiny and angry red, and the blisters had begun along the knuckles. "Sugar sticks. A scald runs off. This didn't run off, it set. You'd have had it on your skin for a while before you got to water."

"A few seconds."

"It's a long few seconds." She said it without comment, and he understood she was telling him he'd been right to cool it and wrong to think it was nothing.

She checked the rest. *Make a fist. Spread your fingers. Touch your thumb to your little finger. Tell me when you feel this.* A light fingertip on each fingertip, one at a time, and he said *yes, yes, yes* and she nodded each time and wrote it down. He had spent his life being the one who noticed the tiny things in a room. He had never been on the receiving end of someone's close attention to his own hand.

"Good," she said. "The cut missed what matters. That's luck."

"Is it?"

"Mostly." She almost smiled. "Zero to ten."

"Six."

"You're a chef. That's an eight." She wrote something. "I'm required to ask what I'm about to ask, so don't take it personally. Alcohol?"

He had been asked in other rooms by other people, and he had learned there was a way to say it that made it small.

"No. Not anymore."

"How long is not anymore?"

He told her the number. She didn't look up, and she didn't make her face do anything.

"Okay. Then here's what I'd offer you for pain, and here's what I'd leave off the table. Which do you want?"

"I'd rather skip anything that makes me like it."

"Then we do it the boring way." She said it as though it were the best answer she'd heard all night. "Numbing for the stitches. Ibuprofen and Tylenol on a schedule, ice and elevation, and you hate me for forty-eight hours. I'll tell the provider." She made a small mark on the chart, and he watched her not underline it.

Behind the curtain, a monitor chirped, and somewhere a gurney rolled. Under the counter by the supply cabinet, a fat pharmacology review book lay open, the spine cracked white. A yellow highlighter stuck out of it like a flag.

"Yours?" Baron asked.

"Nobody else here is dumb enough to study on nights." She flipped it shut with her foot. "I'm going to be a nurse practitioner. Not yet. Right now I can wash this, dress this, and hold the light. Closing that hand," she said, and nodded at the cut, "is not in my scope, and it won't be for a while. I could do it in my sleep, though. Don't tell anyone."

"Who would I tell?"

"My charge nurse, mainly." This time the smile did arrive, quick and tired and real, and it changed her whole face in a way he filed and would not retrieve for several days.

---

The provider was a physician assistant with the eyes of a person on his ninth shift in a row. He numbed the hand, irrigated it, and closed the cut in six neat stitches while Kay held the light and the wrist steady and talked. It was a nurse's trick, he realized. It gave the patient somewhere else to look.

"What's the dish?"

"Redfish. Skin-on. Finished in the oven."

"With a sauce that came for you."

"With a glaze. I call it the Elixir."

"That's a good name for a thing that sends someone to the ER." The PA, concentrating, did not look up. "What's in it?"

Baron opened his mouth, and for the first time in a long time he heard himself about to answer: *tomato, guajillo, Peychaud's*—

He stopped.

"It's never been written down," he said.

"So it can't be stolen," Kay said, looking at the stitches, not at him.

"That's the idea."

"Then I won't ask again." She didn't, and he found the not-asking an unexpectedly intimate thing.

They dressed the burn in layers. A thin, cool silver-colored gauze over the blisters, a loose wrap over that, a splint to keep the palm from bending the stitches. They gave him a tetanus shot because, Kay said, a chef's knife has seen things. The PA went off to chart. Kay stayed to write up the instructions, and she wrote them by hand, on the back of the printed sheet, in neat block capitals.

*NO KITCHEN FOR TWO WEEKS.*

"Two weeks," Baron said.

"That's if you're good."

"I'm not good at being out of a kitchen."

"I figured." She underlined it. "Keep it dry. Keep it up. If the burn weeps yellow, if the fingers go white or numb, if it starts to smell wrong, come back. Stitches out in ten to fourteen days." She tore the sheet from the pad, and then, instead of handing it over, she turned it and wrote one more line at the bottom.

*BURN RECHECK — THURS. ASK FOR KAY.*

"It's the second day that tells you what you really have," she said. "The first night, it all looks like a story. By Thursday, it's the truth. I want to be the one who saw it at the start." She held out the paper. "I'm on nights. Same as you, I'd guess."

"Same as me," Baron said. He took the sheet in his right hand, careful not to look at the way she'd looked at his left.

"Dorgenois," he said. He'd been wanting to say it for a while. "Like the street."

"Like the street." She had heard it ten thousand times and answered it with perfect good humor. "Depends which uncle you ask whether it's named for us or we're named for it."

"Which do you believe?"

"I believe it's a very long street," Kay said, "and I know every block of it."

---

At a quarter to seven, the sky outside the ambulance bay had gone the color of dishwater going pale. Baron stood in the doorway with his left hand propped in its splint against his chest and the instruction sheet folded in his right.

Theron had fallen asleep in the truck, forehead against the glass, the Elixir stain stiff on his sleeve. Baron looked at his cousin through the windshield and thought, for the first time since he'd come home, that the restaurant he'd built was in better hands than he'd ever admitted to himself.

*Two weeks.* The ovens would hold their number without him. The envelope would keep on the rail. The line would run to Theron's timing, and for fourteen days it would be no business of his hands.

He had done it to himself, and he was clear about that. A cook spends a breath, and a pan lips, and a knife is where a knife should never be. It wasn't hers. She had only sung a song, and it was a good song. He would have said so to anyone who'd asked.

But he could still hear it. Not the words, which he hadn't caught, and not the arrangement, which he'd never heard past the first four bars. Only the horn line, behind the beat, looping in his head the way a tune does in the hour after a very long night.

Years ago a voice off a side street had made him stop on a sidewalk and write a note in a notebook. He had gone looking for it for months. This morning it had come to him in his own kitchen without his asking, and cost him six stitches and two weeks, and he found that the strangest part was how little he wished it undone.

He stood there until the bay lights went out on their own. Across the lot, a woman in navy scrubs was walking to a car with a paper cup of coffee in one hand and a cracked textbook under her arm. She didn't look back. He wouldn't have wanted her to.

The smell of the coffee reached him across the cold air, dark and burnt-sweet. For one second he was seventeen again, behind a counter on Decatur Street with a portafilter in his hand, and the whole city was still ahead of him.

Out past the levee, the river had reached the point in the night when the water stops deciding. It was not coming in and it was not going out. It was only waiting to find out which.

By Thursday it would have to.

I took that to mean Baron comes home to a restaurant that doesn't need him. That gives his comeback something to push against, though it isn't spelled out in canon.
2. **The sealed envelope from a law office, behind the ticket rail.** I invented it to hint at the real threat to Bayouboujee, a lease problem that echoes Kaldi's. The land purchase hasn't happened on the page in either book. If the threat is something else, the envelope goes or changes. If the conflict is the land, this is the seed.
3. **The injury details.** The sauce is the Scarlet Elixir, the radio song pulls his eyes away, and the cut is a slice. All follow your decisions. I added that it's his **left** hand, the one that guides the knife, so he can still hold a knife with his right hand later but can't work two weeks. I also added that his own knife was left edge-out on the board, a lapse he'd never allow in a line cook. If you'd rather it be the right hand, the stakes go up.
4. **The song "Slack Tide" is invented.** It has a two-line sample lyric, and it is not a real song. It's dignified and has no blame. It's a song Baron hasn't heard before, which answers the open "which song" question my way: he's avoided her records, but radio can't be avoided.
