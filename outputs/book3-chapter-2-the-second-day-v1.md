# Chapter 2: The Second Day

A man who cannot work his hands discovers how much of his mind lives in them.

For two days Baron learned this the way you learn any truth you'd rather not have: slowly, and in the body. The splint held his left hand in a position that was neither a fist nor a flat, a shape with no use, and every instinct he had went on reaching for work that the hand could not do. He woke at four, the hour his body had kept for twenty years, and lay in the dark of the rented house with nothing to get up for. He made coffee one-handed and spilled the grounds. He could not tie an apron. He could not hold a pan and a spoon at once, which was the whole grammar of a kitchen, the thing you did ten thousand times a night without thinking, and the not-being-able-to sat on him like a weight on the chest.

Theron called twice a day with the numbers. Covers, voids, the fish order, the walk-in temp. He called them in like a sous reporting to a chef, and Baron understood that his cousin was doing it on purpose, to give him a shift to stand at even from a mile away, and he was grateful and it did not help. A report is not a kitchen. You cannot smell a report. By eight o'clock you do not know from a report whether you won.

The envelope he did not call about. It sat on the ticket rail at the restaurant, and it sat in his mind at the house, and the two of them had an understanding now, the envelope and Baron, the way a man and a thing he is avoiding come to an understanding. He knew the return address. He had known it since Friday. A property management company on Poydras, the kind that does not write to you with good news, and under its name a line he had been not-reading for six days: *RE: 2000 lease renewal — notice of material change.*

He knew what material change meant. He had learned it standing on a sidewalk on Decatur Street twenty-odd years ago, looking up at forty-foot ceilings that had forgotten what they were for. Material change meant the number was going up. Material change meant someone upstream had done the math and decided the dirt under his restaurant was worth more as a decimal than as a thing anyone had built.

He would open it when his hand worked. That was the deal he had made with himself, and he knew it was a child's deal, and he kept it anyway.

---

Thursday came the way the city brought things, on its own schedule and with no ceremony. He drove himself to the hospital with his right hand, which the splint permitted and which felt like cheating, and he arrived too early the way he arrived too early for everything, and he sat in the same low waiting room under the same floor-wax air and did not read the television.

He had told himself, on the drive, several reasonable things. That a recheck was a recheck. That she had written *ask for Kay* because continuity of care was good medicine and not because of anything else, and that a man of fifty who read anything else into the handwriting of a tired nurse at one in the morning was a man who had been alone with his own kitchen for too long. He had told himself these things in Baron's own voice, which was calm, and precise, and devoid of panic, and which did not entirely believe itself.

"Vieux."

She said it the city's way again. Black scrubs tonight, or today — the hospital kept its own weather, and he'd lost track of which the sky was doing. The stethoscope, the badge, the hair pulled back the same severe neat. She looked less tired than she had, or more rested, which was not the same thing and he noticed the difference the way he noticed everything.

"You came back," she said.

"You wrote it down. I'm good at following a prep list."

"That's the first true thing a chef has ever told me in this building." She tipped her head toward the hall. "Come on. Let's see what you really have."

---

She unwound the splint and the wrap in the bright of the exam light, slow, her thumb and two fingers doing the work, and he watched her read his hand the way he'd watched her read it the first night, and he made himself look at the wound instead of at her reading of it.

It had gone the colors it was supposed to go. The burn along the knuckles had dried and darkened at the edges and the blisters had not opened, which she said aloud, *blisters are intact, that's what we want*, in the flat, certain register he was coming to recognize as the sound of her telling him good news without letting him make too much of it. The cut on the heel of the hand had closed clean around its six stitches. No heat where there shouldn't be heat. No weeping. No red climbing the wrist.

"This is a good second day," she said. "You cooled it fast that night. Whoever did that saved you a skin graft conversation."

"My cousin did it. I trained him."

"Then you trained him well." She pressed, gently, at points along the burn, watching his face and not his hand. "Tell me when it's sharp instead of sore."

He told her. She mapped it the way he mapped a plate, each point in its place, nothing approximate.

"You do that like a cook," he said.

She didn't look up. "Do what."

"Work in exact amounts. Most people press on a burn like they're apologizing to it. You press like you're measuring."

Now she looked up. Something moved across her face that was not quite a smile, the thing a person does when you've seen the one part of them they thought they'd kept out of the room.

"My mother cooked," she said, after a moment, and went back to the hand. "Not like you, I'd bet. Church-hall, feed-forty-people, know-where-every-pot-is cooking. I used to do the mise for her before I had a word for it. Everything in its bowl before you turn on a single eye." She re-seated the gauze. "You don't stop knowing how to do that. You just stop calling it cooking and start calling it whatever your actual job is."

"And your actual job is—"

"Keeping people alive on the worst night of their lives, mostly. And studying, when it's slow." She secured the wrap. "There. Better than it has any right to be. Stitches out Monday, but that's not the thing I want to say to you."

"There's a thing."

"There's always a thing. The burn's healing. The hand will be a hand again." She sat back on the little rolling stool and looked at him level, and the professional register dropped by exactly one degree, the way a stock stops roiling and goes to a simmer. "What I want to say is: don't rush back behind that stove the day the splint comes off. The skin'll be new and thin and it won't tell you it's burning until it's already burned. You'll trust the hand before the hand is trustworthy. Chefs always do. I've stitched enough of you to know."

"Enough of me."

"Line cooks, Sunday grillers, a man who tried to debone a turkey with a box cutter once. You all come in sure it was a fluke." She stood. "It's never a fluke. It's always the one second you looked away."

He said nothing to that, because there was nothing to say to a true thing, and because the one second he had looked away had a voice and a horn line and a name he was not going to put in this room.

She caught the nothing. She was, he was learning, a person who caught the nothing.

"What were you looking at?" she asked. Not pressing. Just the question, set down on the table between them like a cup.

"The oven," Baron said.

"That's where your eyes were supposed to be. I asked what they went to."

And he found, to his own surprise, that he wanted to answer her. Not the whole of it — not the five years, not the South American coast, not the platinum records or the quiet it had taken to become a man who could hear her on a radio and keep breathing. But something.

"A song came on," he said. "On the radio in the kitchen. I used to know the person singing it. I hadn't heard the song before. I turned my head to hear it better." He looked at his bandaged hand. "That's the whole crime."

Kay was quiet for a moment. Whatever she had expected, it hadn't been that, and she had the grace not to pretend otherwise.

"Must've been some song," she said, finally.

"It was a good song," Baron said. "I'd have told anyone that."

She wrote something on his chart. He couldn't see what, and he had the sense, which he did not examine, that it had nothing to do with his hand.

---

She walked him out the long way, past the supply room where the cracked pharmacology textbook still lay on the counter with its yellow flag of highlighter, and he nodded at it.

"Still at it."

"NP program starts me in January, if the financial aid comes through and the universe doesn't do anything cute." She said it lightly, but he heard the weight under the lightness, the way he heard the weight under everything. "Two more years after that. Then I can do more than hold the light while somebody else closes the wound I already know how to close."

"You said that the first night. That you could've stitched me in your sleep."

"I could have."

"I believe you."

"That's the thing about where I am right now," Kay said, and stopped at the doors, the gray morning or evening pressing against the glass behind her. "I know how to do the thing. I'm just not allowed to sign my name to it yet. There's a version of that that makes you bitter, and I've met the nurses who went that way, and I decided a long time ago I wasn't going to be one of them. But it's a daily decision. It doesn't make itself."

Baron thought about a man in a gray coat standing at the pass of a restaurant with his own name stitched on the chest, running stations nobody had asked him to run, because the thing he knew how to do better than anything in the world was now, correctly and by his own design, somebody else's to sign for.

"I know the version that makes you bitter," he said. "I'm standing in it this week."

She looked at him then, really looked, the reading look turned on the whole of him instead of his hand.

"Yeah," she said. "I figured you might."

---

He did not open the envelope that night either. But he drove back to the restaurant for the first time since the injury, after close, when the lot had cleared and the ovens had gone to standby, and he let himself in with the key he still carried and stood in the quiet of a kitchen that had run perfectly well without him.

The pan he'd dropped was long since washed and racked. Someone had wiped down the board and turned the knives edge-in to the wall, the way the rule said, the way he had looked away from his own rule for one second and paid for it. Theron ran a clean house. He'd known that. It was different to stand inside the proof of it with a splint on his hand.

The radio was on the shelf above dry storage, silent now, a scratched forty-dollar thing that had cost him six stitches and two weeks and something he didn't have a splint for.

And the envelope was on the rail, where it had been for nine days.

He looked at it a long time. Then he took it down, one-handed, and held it, and did not open it, and understood that the reason was not his hand. His hand could have managed. The reason was that the last time a landlord had decided a place was worth more as a number, Baron had been a boy on a sidewalk with no power to do anything but grieve it, and some part of him that had never fully grown past that sidewalk did not want to read the sentence that would tell him whether the thing was going to happen to him again, to the one place he had built precisely so that it could never happen to him again.

*You can't double the rent on something you own.*

He had said that to himself for twenty years. He had built the whole of Bayouboujee on the back of that sentence.

He turned the envelope over in the lamplight. *Notice of material change.*

He had leased the land first. That had always been the plan, the first step, the thing you did before you owned — prove the concept, make the numbers, then buy the ground out from under the people who could otherwise raise it on you. He'd meant to buy it years ago. Then there had been the crisis, and the recovery, and the leaving, and five years on a coast on the far side of the world, and the restaurant had kept running under Theron's hand and had kept, he'd assumed, paying its rent into an account he had stopped watching closely because everything had been fine.

Everything had been fine.

The lease ran out in 2000. No — the lease he'd signed ran to its ten-year term and would run out this year, and the renewal was the envelope in his hand, and he had not been home to see the first step turn, quietly, while he wasn't watching, back into the only step. He had never bought the land. He had always been going to. You can't double the rent on something you own, and he had never gotten around to owning it, and now there was an envelope.

He set it back on the rail.

He would open it when his hand worked. He knew it was a child's deal. He kept it one more night, because a man is allowed, once in his life, to stand in the kitchen he built with his name on the wall and not yet know for certain whether he is about to lose it.

Outside, somewhere across the city, the night kept its own schedule. And on the second day, which is the day that tells you what you really have, Baron Vieux learned two true things and let himself feel only one of them.

The hand would heal.

The other thing he folded up and put away, the way she had folded the instruction sheet, and carried out into the dark.
